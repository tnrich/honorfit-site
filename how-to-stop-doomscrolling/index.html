How to stop doomscrolling

Without deleting every app and reinstalling it four days later.

Doomscrolling isn't a discipline problem, which is why treating it like one keeps failing. The feed is built by very good engineers to find the exact thing that keeps your thumb moving, and it learns from you every session. Trying to out-willpower it at 11pm, tired, isn't a fair fight, and you already know how it ends.

What works is changing the terms so you don't have to win that fight. Here's what I'd do, roughly in order. (I make an app for step four, so grain of salt. The rest works with any app, or none.)

The short version

  • Find out which app it really is.
  • Stop relying on limits you can dismiss with one tap.
  • Make the phone harder to reach on autopilot.
  • Put a small, real price on opening the app.
  • Only spend time while you're actually in the app.
  • Have something else within reach.
  • Give it two weeks, then adjust.

1. Notice which app it actually is

Most people guess wrong. Check your screen time report before you do anything else (Settings › Screen Time on iPhone, Digital Wellbeing on Android). The fix for a two-hour YouTube habit is different from the fix for forty quick Instagram checks a day. Block the one that shows up, not the one you feel guilty about.

2. Stop relying on a limit you can dismiss

Built-in app timers fail for a simple reason: the “Ignore Limit” button is right there, and pressing it costs nothing. After about a week it's muscle memory, and you're past the screen before you've decided anything. Any block you can skip with one tap eventually becomes a block with one extra tap.

3. Attack the trigger, not the session

Almost every scroll starts the same way: an idle second, a hand reaching for the phone, an icon in the same spot it's always been. Cheap, boring changes to that path do real work. Move the app off your home screen and out of the dock, turn off every notification that isn't from a real person, and charge your phone in another room overnight. If most of your scrolling happens in bed, your phone's built-in Downtime or bedtime schedule is a good free tool for that part.

4. Make opening the app cost something

This is the piece the other tactics are missing. Friction only holds if the price is real, and the most reliable real price is physical. Twenty pushups to unlock twenty minutes of Instagram isn't a punishment. It's an exchange rate. Some of the time you'll pay it, and that's fine. The rest of the time you'll put the phone down, because it turns out you didn't want it enough to get on the floor.

Either way you win, which is why this beats a blocker that only ever frustrates you. You're not trying to never open Instagram again. You're trying to make opening it a decision instead of a reflex.

5. Spend the earned time on purpose

If your earned minutes drain while the phone is face-down on a table, you'll stop trusting the system. Time should only be spent while you're actually in the app. Then your balance means what it says, and running out means something.

6. Have something else within reach

A lot of doomscrolling is just filling a gap: waiting for the kettle, five minutes before a meeting, lying in bed not tired yet. If the phone is the only thing within reach, it wins. Put a book on the nightstand, keep a crossword by the couch, go for a short walk. Boring stuff. It just has to be easier to grab than the feed.

7. Give it two weeks, and expect to renegotiate

The first few days are uncomfortable, and you'll reach for the phone constantly out of habit. That fades. What usually needs adjusting is the exchange rate: too harsh and you'll uninstall the blocker, too generous and nothing changes. Start with something reasonable, like a minute per pushup, and tune it after the first week based on how you actually behaved.

FAQ

Why do I doomscroll even when I'm not enjoying it?

Feeds are designed to keep serving you something new, and once scrolling is a habit, your hand reaches for the phone before you've decided anything. That's why the fixes that work change the habit's path instead of relying on willpower in the moment.

Is deleting the apps the best way to stop doomscrolling?

It works for some people, but a lot of people reinstall within days, especially apps they also use for messages or work. Making the app cost something to open is a middle ground that's easier for many people to stick with.

Do screen time limits work for doomscrolling?

They're great for seeing where your time goes and for time-of-day rules like bedtime. They're weak against a strong habit, because the Ignore Limit button costs nothing to press.

How long does it take to break a doomscrolling habit?

There's no fixed number. Give any change at least two weeks before you judge it, and expect to adjust your rules after the first week.
